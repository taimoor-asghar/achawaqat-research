Limitations & common pitfalls

  • Screening, not diagnosis. A positive MoCA score is not equivalent to a diagnosis. Confirmatory clinical evaluation is required before treatment decisions.
  • Population calibration. Cutoffs were derived from specific validation populations. Performance can differ in other age groups, languages, comorbid conditions, or clinical settings.
  • Self-report bias. Patient-reported instruments are subject to under- and over-reporting; clinical context is essential.

Frequently asked questions about MoCA

What does MoCA measure?

MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment) is a validated instrument that assesses cognitive screening. Its primary clinical use is Cognitive screening, especially for mild cognitive impairment. It takes about 10 minutes to complete, and the items are combined to produce a total score.

How long does MoCA take to complete?

MoCA typically takes about 10 minutes to administer. The exact duration varies a little depending on whether it is self-administered or completed with a clinician. Its brief format is part of what makes it practical for routine clinical use and for repeat administration when tracking change over time.

Why isn't MoCA scored interactively here?

MoCA requires user certification (paid) to administer since September 2020.

Is there a free alternative to MoCA?

Yes — SLUMS. Free cognitive screen developed by Saint Louis University; comparable sensitivity for MCI.

What is the source paper for MoCA?

Nasreddine ZS, Phillips NA, Bédirian V, et al. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA: a brief screening tool for mild cognitive impairment. J Am Geriatr Soc. 2005;53(4):695-699. This is the original publication for the MoCA — the reference to cite when reporting results.

Can MoCA replace clinical judgment?

No. MoCA is a structured assessment aid, not a diagnostic test. Its proper role is Cognitive screening, especially for mild cognitive impairment — it standardizes assessment and flags who needs closer evaluation, but the result must be weighed alongside history, examination, and clinical context. When a score conflicts with your clinical impression, clinical judgment takes precedence; treatment decisions should never rest on a screening score alone.
