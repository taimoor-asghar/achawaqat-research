Clinical context: when sPESI is used

Like all screening or assessment instruments, sPESI is a structured aid — not a diagnostic test in isolation. Results should be interpreted alongside history, examination, and clinical context. Where a score crosses an actionable threshold, the next step is typically a more detailed clinical evaluation rather than a definitive diagnosis.

How sPESI is scored

Limitations & common pitfalls

  • Educational use only.
  • Screening, not diagnosis. A positive sPESI score is not equivalent to a diagnosis. Confirmatory clinical evaluation is required before treatment decisions.
  • Population calibration. Cutoffs were derived from specific validation populations. Performance can differ in other age groups, languages, comorbid conditions, or clinical settings.
  • Self-report bias. Patient-reported instruments are subject to under- and over-reporting; clinical context is essential.

Frequently asked questions about sPESI

What does sPESI measure?

sPESI (Simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index) is a validated instrument that assesses 30-day mortality risk after acute PE. It is designed for clinicians, medical students, evidence-synthesis researchers. The 6 items are combined to produce a total score.

How many items are on sPESI?

sPESI contains 6 items. Item scores are combined with weighted scoring to produce the total. Scores run from 0 to 6, grouped into 2 interpretation bands from ‘Low risk’ up to ‘High risk’.

What is a high sPESI score?

On the sPESI (total score 0–6), scores of 1–6 fall in the ‘High risk’ band — interpreted as: Higher 30-day mortality risk. The full ladder is: 0 — Low risk: Low 30-day mortality risk.; 1–6 — High risk: Higher 30-day mortality risk. A higher score reflects a more severe presentation, and crossing a cutoff is a prompt to step up assessment or care — but these bands are screening thresholds, not diagnoses.

What is a low sPESI score?

Scores of 0 fall in the ‘Low risk’ band: Low 30-day mortality risk. A low score is reassuring but does not by itself rule out the underlying condition — if symptoms or clinical suspicion persist, reassess over time or use a complementary evaluation rather than relying on the number alone.

Is sPESI free to use?

Yes — sPESI is in the public domain and free for clinical, educational, and research use without permission. You can use this calculator and the scale itself freely; if you publish results or reproduce the scale, cite the original source paper.

What is the source paper for sPESI?

Jiménez D et al. Arch Intern Med. 2010;170(15):1383-1389. This is the original publication for the sPESI — the reference to cite when reporting results.

Can sPESI replace clinical judgment?

No. sPESI is a structured assessment aid, not a diagnostic test. Its proper role is clinical assessment of 30-day mortality risk after acute PE — it standardizes assessment and flags who needs closer evaluation, but the result must be weighed alongside history, examination, and clinical context. When a score conflicts with your clinical impression, clinical judgment takes precedence; treatment decisions should never rest on a screening score alone.
